01
THE PROBLEM

Missing-middle housing — duplexes, triplexes, courtyard apartments, small multi-unit buildings mixed into single-family blocks — almost certainly exists in greater quantity than official county records show, because across most of El Paso it is not legal to build. Single-family zoning covers the overwhelming majority of residential land, so an informal second unit, a converted garage, or a backyard casita has every incentive to stay off the books. A property owner who adds a unit doesn't file a permit for something zoning forbids.

That means the central appraisal district's own classification — built from permitted, assessed improvements — is a floor, not a ceiling, on how much missing-middle housing actually exists. The rest of this page sizes that gap from five independent angles: EPCAD's own baseline data, a structural model trained to spot what EPCAD's own classification might miss, an external geometric check against real building footprints, an external survey check against Census data, and an external address-level check against El Paso Electric's own meter billing records.

BOTTOM LINE, UP FRONT
EPCAD's own records say 21.56% of El Paso's housing units are missing-middle — but most of that is actually large apartment complexes, not missing middle in the term's real sense. Scoped correctly (2-19 units, the definition Parolek and the paper this project adapts both use), EPCAD's figure drops to 10.11%, while the Census's own household survey (ACS) implies 16.96% actually exists. EPCAD's classification — a legal record of what's been permitted and assessed — captures only about 60% of the missing-middle housing independently gathered survey data says is really there, at that scoped definition. Section 5 has the full comparison; section 7 ties all five pieces of evidence together.

BASELINE DATA — HOW EPCAD CLASSIFIES HOUSING

El Paso Central Appraisal District (EPCAD) already publishes an authoritative, deterministic typology per parcel from its STATE_CD/ClassCD fields — no model needed to read it. Mapped and broken down below: what EPCAD itself already says is missing-middle, where it concentrates, and how that composition shifts by era and zoning district.

WHERE TRIPLEXES AND FOURPLEXES ACTUALLY LIVE IN THIS DATA

EPCAD's STATE_CD does track them separately — codes B3 and B4 — but an earlier version of this pipeline lumped B3, B4, B5, and B6 together into a single "Multi-Unit Residential (Other)" bucket and, worse, counted every parcel in that bucket as exactly one unit in every unit-weighted statistic on this page. A real triplex was being counted the same as a studio apartment. That's now fixed: B3 is split out as Triplex (368 parcels, 3 units each) and B4 as Fourplex (906 parcels, 4 units each) — based on the Texas statewide SPTB state-code convention other Texas appraisal districts document for these same codes, corroborated here by B3/B4 parcels averaging more improvement records per parcel than B1 duplexes do. B5 (125 parcels) and B6 (24) stay lumped as "Multi-Unit Residential (Other)" — too few parcels and too ambiguous a structural signal to confidently assign them a specific unit count (EPCAD's own site blocks automated lookups, so this couldn't be confirmed against a published legend directly).

Fixing this wasn't cosmetic — this page's headline undercount numbers moved. Countywide unit-weighted missing-middle share went from 20.72% to 21.56%; at the scoped 2-19-unit definition (section 5), EPCAD's recorded share went from 9.15% to 10.11% of what ACS implies exists, closing roughly a point of the undercount gap just by counting units in parcels that were already correctly flagged as missing-middle, just under-weighted.

THE MODEL — LEARNING WHAT MISSING MIDDLE LOOKS LIKE STRUCTURALLY

A classifier trained only on confirmed missing-middle and confirmed single-family parcels — 37 features across four groups (structural: lot size, structure count, square footage, year built, distance downtown; building-shape/footprint; ownership/financing, including First American's independent unit/building counts; and physical capacity, specifically First American's total bathroom count — a house needs enough existing plumbing to split before it's worth converting), with the EPCAD code that defines the label explicitly excluded — learns what missing-middle housing looks like structurally, then flags single-family-labeled parcels that fit the same signature. Test AUC: 0.997. Adding the shape, ownership, and bathroom-count features (an earlier pass used structural features alone) raised AUC from 0.986, but also shrank the candidate set it flags — a more precise classifier ends up more conservative, not more generous. See the callout below the candidate panel for what that traded off. Below are the descriptive statistics behind why it works, and what it flags.

SHARE BY LOT SIZE

Not a straight line — a U-shape. Share runs 47.8% on the smallest lots (under 1,500 sqft), bottoms out around 1.6% at the typical single-family lot size (4,500-8,700 sqft), then climbs to 41.2% on 87,100+ sqft parcels (2+ acres — large multi-family sites). The linear correlation alone (r = +0.197) would miss this shape entirely.

Click a bar to see which typologies make up that lot-size bucket.

SHARE BY BUILDING SIZE

Same U-shape: 10.1% share at 600-900 sqft (casita-sized), down to 2.3% at 1500-1800 sqft, back up to 16.7% at 3500+ sqft (large apartment buildings). The linear read (r = +0.068) is weak and misleading on its own.

ZONING CODES MOST CONCENTRATED

Share of parcels within each code that are missing-middle (min. 100 parcels), using the City of El Paso's official zoning layer (clean ZONELABEL), not EPCAD's raw GIS field, which had inconsistent duplicate spellings for the same district.

OWNERSHIP SIGNAL — HOMESTEAD EXEMPTION

Texas's homestead exemption legally requires owner-occupancy — the strongest single correlate of missing-middle status found this round (r = -0.27).

THREE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS, THREE DIFFERENT (AND NOT CONTRADICTORY) ANSWERS

It's easy to read "0.997 AUC" next to "0.66 precision" and hear the model described as both great and not-great at the same time. It isn't a contradiction — they're answers to three separate questions, and all three can be true at once:

Q1 · CAN IT TELL THEM APART AT ALL?

Show it one confirmed missing-middle parcel and one confirmed single-family parcel, no threshold involved — just rank them. It gets the ranking right 99.7% of the time (AUC). This is the model's raw ability to tell the two apart, independent of any yes/no decision.

Q2 · WHERE DO YOU DRAW THE LINE?

To turn that ranking into an actual candidate list, you have to pick a cutoff. This project uses p ≥ 0.5 — a deliberately loose bar, chosen to flag more parcels worth a second look rather than fewer. A stricter cutoff (p ≥ 0.8) produces a shorter, higher-confidence list instead; see the candidate panel below.

Q3 · HOW GOOD IS THE LIST AT THAT LINE?

At p ≥ 0.5: 95% recall (it catches 95% of true missing-middle parcels in the test set) but only 66% precision (1 in 3 flagged parcels is a false positive). A loose cutoff chosen to maximize recall will always cost precision — that's not a flaw in the model, it's the direct, mechanical consequence of the choice made in Q2.

THE ANALOGY

Think of a smoke detector tuned to go off at the faintest hint of smoke. It will catch nearly every real fire (high recall) — but it will also go off for burnt toast fairly often (lower precision). That doesn't mean the detector is bad at sensing smoke; a less sensitive detector would miss more real fires in exchange for fewer false alarms. Which trade-off is "right" depends on what the alarm is for. Here, the candidate list is a screening tool meant to flag parcels worth a closer look, not a final determination — so this project deliberately tunes toward catching more real missing-middle parcels, accepting the false-positive cost that comes with it, rather than a tighter cutoff that would miss more of the real ones to avoid flagging a few extra.

One more thing none of these three numbers answer: "did the model find real undercounted missing-middle housing." AUC, precision, and recall are all scored against EPCAD's own labels — parcels the county has already classified one way or the other. The candidates below are single-family-labeled parcels the model thinks share the missing-middle signature; EPCAD has made no determination on them, so there's no ground truth to score against directly. Section 5's ACS comparison is the closest this project gets to an answer on that separate question — and the answer there is much more modest than the AUC alone would suggest.

CANDIDATE DETECTOR — WHAT GIVES IT AWAY

Scored against every single-family-labeled parcel, the model flags lower-confidence and at ≥80% confidence as candidates the county's own classification may have missed (test AUC ) — shown on the map above as hollow markers.

BETTER CLASSIFIER, SMALLER CANDIDATE SET

Adding footprint-shape and ownership/financing features (First American's independent unit/building counts among them) raised test AUC from 0.986 to 0.996 and sharply improved precision — but also tightened the model's probability estimates, so fewer parcels clear each confidence threshold: candidates at p≥0.5 fell from 7,909 to 4,439, and high-confidence candidates (p≥0.8) fell from 2,622 to 1,569. Downstream, that smaller set closes slightly less of the section 5 EPCAD-vs-ACS gap than the noisier prior model did (88 of 182 tracts improved vs. 96 of 182). A classifier that's better at telling confirmed missing-middle from confirmed single-family apart isn't automatically better at guessing at the undercounted kind EPCAD never labeled at all — those are different questions, and this project reports both answers rather than the more flattering one.

The current model adds one more field on top of that v2 baseline: First American's total bathroom count — a physical-capacity signal (a house needs enough existing plumbing to split before a conversion is worth doing), ranking #5 in feature importance. It nudges test AUC to 0.997 and shrinks the candidate set a bit further (4,439 → 4,072 at p≥0.5; 1,569 → 1,471 at p≥0.8), and on the section 5 downstream test it's essentially a wash (84 of 182 tracts improved either way, mean diff 10.22 → 10.19). It's kept anyway: the ACS tract comparison only tests whether a parcel looks undercounted today, not whether it's physically capable of being converted tomorrow — a question the bathroom count speaks to that the downstream test was never built to measure.

VALIDATION, LAYER 1 — BUILDING FOOTPRINTS (EXTERNAL, GEOMETRIC)

The original Salazar-Miranda & Talen paper detects housing typology from a street-view CV model's read of building shape. El Paso has no imagery pipeline, but real building-footprint polygons (Microsoft GlobalMLBuildingFootprints) let us compute actual shape features and cross-check EPCAD's own structure counts against physical reality — independent of the classifier above.

FOOTPRINTS VS. RECORDED STRUCTURES

Of single-family parcels with footprint coverage, show more dwelling-sized footprints than EPCAD has recorded structures (a candidate undocumented ADU/casita), and show footprint area exceeding recorded square footage by >1.5x (a candidate undocumented addition). Caveat: footprint polygons are noisy (split roofs, oversized garages) — read this as a screening signal, not a confirmed count.

Broken out by typology (medians, since a few shared-building parcels skew the mean): duplexes run hot on the footprint-ratio signal (25.0% addition-flag rate vs. 2.3% for single-family) without the extreme outliers seen elsewhere — a real structural signature, not noise. Large apartment complexes (31-99 / 100+ units) show high new-detached rates (22% / 42%), expected since big complexes legitimately have many buildings per parcel. Townhouse/condo addition-flag rates (44% / 49%) are mostly a spatial-join artifact — a shared building's footprint sometimes attaches to one unit's small individually-recorded parcel — not real undocumented expansions; see data/shape_by_typology.json.

VALIDATION, LAYER 2 — ACS UNITS-IN-STRUCTURE (EXTERNAL, SURVEY-BASED)

The Census Bureau's ACS asks households directly how many units are in their building (table B25024), independent of any local permitting or assessment process — a third benchmark alongside EPCAD's parcel records and the footprint cross-check above. Whose count is closer to reality?

EPCAD VS. ACS UNITS-IN-STRUCTURE, BY CENSUS TRACT

Each dot is one of 182 census tracts. The chart below plots EPCAD's broad (any non-single-family structure) share against ACS — countywide, EPCAD (21.56%) and ACS (23.05%) look close, but that's still somewhat misleading: EPCAD's broad flag includes large apartment complexes that aren't "missing middle" by any real definition, and the ACS side was scoped the same broad way to match it.

Scoped to missing middle's actual definition — 2-19 units, per Parolek and the Salazar-Miranda & Talen paper this project adapts — the picture changes substantially. EPCAD (10.11%) captures only about 60% of what ACS implies exists (16.96%) — a real 6.85-point countywide undercount, not a unit-of-analysis artifact (down from a 7.8-point/54% gap before this project split EPCAD's B3/B4 "triplex"/"fourplex" parcels out of a catch-all "Multi-Unit Residential (Other)" bucket that had been counting every one of them as a single unit — see section 2 for that fix). Tract-level mean absolute difference is 10.22 points. Either way it's weak evidence, not decisive, and nowhere near enough to close a gap this size (ACS implies ~21,000 more missing-middle units exist than EPCAD has on record, down from ~24,000 before the B3/B4 fix). VALIDATION, LAYER 3 — ELECTRIC METER BILLING CLASS (EXTERNAL, ADDRESS-LEVEL)

El Paso Electric classifies every service address into a billing class — Residential Single, Residential Duplex, Residential Triplex, or Apartment Complex — for rate-setting purposes, entirely independent of EPCAD's parcel records or this project's model. Unlike the footprint and ACS checks above, which only validate aggregate counts, this is the first source that can be joined address-by-address to the structural model's specific flagged parcels.

DOES EPE INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRM THE MODEL'S FLAGGED PARCELS?

The join itself: EPE's data has no parcel ID or coordinates — only a free-text service address and ZIP — so this is a normalized-address string join against EPCAD's situs address fields, not a guaranteed-unique key match like the other validations in this project. 58.8% of EPCAD's residential parcels (145,094 of 246,950) matched to an EPE residential meter record; the rest didn't match, most likely because an address didn't normalize identically on both sides (abbreviations, unit suffixes, EPE coverage gaps for addresses served by another utility), not because no meter exists. Unmatched means "couldn't check," not "not missing middle."

Sanity check first: of the 8,739 parcels EPCAD's own STATE_CD field already calls missing-middle, 4,219 matched to an EPE record, and EPE's independent billing class agrees they're multi-unit (duplex/triplex/apartment, not single) for only 22.8% of them. That's lower than it sounds like it should be, but it has a real, previously-known explanation rather than being a join-quality problem: EPE bills by electric account, not by inspected unit count — a duplex with one owner-paid account for the whole building, or units sub-metered by the landlord rather than EPE, reads as "Residential Single" to EPE even though it's a confirmed two-unit building. The breakdown bears this out directly — of EPCAD-confirmed missing-middle parcels EPE calls "Residential Single," 2,142 are duplexes, 502 are townhouses, and 400 are fourplexes: EPE agreement is a floor on multi-unit status, not the real rate, exactly the same one-directional-undercount pattern as every other source in this project.

The actual test: for the structural model's 1,471 high-confidence (p ≥ 0.8) candidates — EPCAD single-family parcels the model thinks are secretly multi-unit — 775 (52.7%) matched an EPE record, and of those, 128 (16.5%) are independently billed by EPE as duplex, triplex, or apartment-complex rather than single. Among all EPCAD single-family parcels matched to EPE (not just the model's candidates), that rate is 0.76% — the model's high-confidence candidates hit an independent, address-level multi-unit signal at 21.7x the baseline rate. That's a genuinely different kind of validation than anything else in this project: not an aggregate count matching up, but 128 specific parcels where a third, independent administrative system agrees with the model about a specific address.

Caveat, to be direct about it: 16.5% is a floor, not the model's true hit rate — same billing-account undercount logic as above means plenty of real hidden units never show up as a duplex/triplex/apartment EPE class even when they exist. But it cuts the other way too: a 21.7x lift over baseline, from a data source with every incentive to under-, not over-, report multi-unit billing, is hard to explain away as coincidence. It's independent corroboration for a meaningful slice of the model's candidates, not proof for all of them. See data/epe_meter_validation.json and data/epe_confirmed_hidden_units.csv for the full figures and the 128 confirmed addresses.

WHAT THIS MEANS, PUT TOGETHER

Five independent pieces of evidence, each with its own blind spots, point the same direction.

1 · THE LEGAL MECHANISM SECTION 1

Single-family zoning makes most missing-middle housing illegal to build, so owners have every reason not to document it.

2 · EPCAD'S OWN PATTERN SECTIONS 2-3

Missing-middle already concentrates exactly where that mechanism predicts — near downtown, odd lot sizes, older stock, non-owner-occupancy — cleanly enough that a structural model separates it from single-family at 0.997 AUC using structure, shape, ownership, and physical-capacity signal, never the EPCAD label itself.

3 · THE FOOTPRINT CHECK SECTION 4

An entirely separate data source — building-footprint geometry — independently confirms ~8.7% of single-family-labeled parcels carry more or larger structures than EPCAD has on record.

4 · THE ACS CHECK SECTION 5

A third, completely independent source puts a number on the gap: at missing middle's real definition, EPCAD accounts for only about 60% of what household survey data implies exists.

5 · THE ELECTRIC METER CHECK SECTION 6

A fourth independent source, matched address-by-address rather than in aggregate: El Paso Electric's own billing class confirms a duplex/triplex/apartment meter at 21.7x the baseline rate for the model's high-confidence candidates specifically — 128 individually-named parcels where a third administrative system agrees with the model.

TOGETHER, THEY POINT TO ONE EXPLANATION
THE KEY FINDING, REASONED THROUGH

Put those five pieces together and a specific explanation for where the undercount lives falls out — an inference from the pattern of results, not a new measurement on its own. A model trained purely on structure, footprint shape, ownership, and bathroom-count signal — never on EPCAD's own classification — can tell confirmed missing-middle parcels from confirmed single-family parcels apart at 0.997 AUC (section 3): converted missing-middle housing leaves a detectable structural fingerprint even with no permit on file. But two separate exterior-evidence methods built to catch that fingerprint — the footprint-shape check (~8.7% of single-family parcels flagged) and the model's high-confidence candidates (1,471-2,622 parcels across model versions) — both undershoot the ACS-implied ~21,000-unit gap by roughly an order of magnitude.

The straightforward reading: most of the undercounted missing-middle housing was converted without any exterior change — internal subdivisions, a garage or basement turned into a separate unit, no new footprint or roofline — exactly the kind of conversion no satellite comparison or permit record would ever catch. This project has no interior-inspection or permit-matching data to confirm it directly, but it's the explanation consistent with every method here without requiring any of them to be wrong. The electric-meter check (section 6) adds one more data point in the same direction: it independently confirms a real slice (128 parcels, a 21.7x lift over baseline) of the model's candidates specifically, without being able to confirm or rule out the rest — exactly what you'd expect if the candidates are mostly real but EPE's billing-account classification misses most of them the same way every other source here undercounts in one direction.

WHICH MEANS
THREE IMPLICATIONS

First — single-family homes can reasonably be converted to missing-middle housing, but for zoning and building codes. The conversions are evidently feasible, since the pattern above implies they're already happening; what's missing is the legal framework from section 1, not a technical or market barrier.

Second — these renovations don't require changes that would alter a neighborhood's exterior character. If they did, the two exterior-evidence methods would have found far more of them than they did.

Third — this data can help target where zoning and code reform would matter most, because the concentration pattern in sections 2-3 (near downtown, older housing stock, non-owner-occupied) identifies specific tracts where informal conversion is evidently already happening, ahead of any policy change.

WHAT THIS PROJECT DOES NOT SHOW

That the structural candidate detector has found the missing units, or that internal conversion is the only driver of the gap. Some of it is almost certainly EPCAD assessment lag or genuine ACS sampling noise at the tract level — this project can't cleanly separate how much of the ~24,000-unit gap is real but invisible conversion versus measurement noise between two different data sources. Confirming the internal-conversion explanation specifically would take field verification or a different kind of evidence entirely (permit-vs-utility-hookup cross-referencing, interior inspection, resident surveys) that this project's geometry- and classification-based methods can't provide on their own.

FOR POLICY PURPOSES

El Paso almost certainly has substantially more missing-middle housing than its own records show, concentrated in specific, identifiable places — inner-ring, pre-war, non-owner-occupied tracts — and the best-supported explanation for why it's invisible to both administrative records and satellite-based detection is that it's happening inside existing buildings, not through additions or new construction that would already have been visible. Already happening, already low-impact on neighborhood character, already concentrated in identifiable places — that's the case for treating this as a legalization and code-reform question rather than a detection problem to keep refining.
