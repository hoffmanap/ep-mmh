01
THE PROBLEM

Missing-middle housing — duplexes, triplexes, courtyard apartments, small multi-unit buildings mixed into single-family blocks — almost certainly exists in greater quantity than official county records show, because across most of El Paso it is not legal to build. Single-family zoning covers the overwhelming majority of residential land, so an informal second unit, a converted garage, or a backyard casita has every incentive to stay off the books. A property owner who adds a unit doesn't file a permit for something zoning forbids.

That means the central appraisal district's own classification — built from permitted, assessed improvements — is a floor, not a ceiling, on how much missing-middle housing actually exists. The rest of this page sizes that gap from four independent angles: EPCAD's own baseline data, a structural model trained to spot what EPCAD's own classification might miss, an external geometric check against real building footprints, and an external survey check against Census data.

BOTTOM LINE, UP FRONT
EPCAD's own records say 20.72% of El Paso's housing units are missing-middle — but most of that is actually large apartment complexes, not missing middle in the term's real sense. Scoped correctly (2-19 units, the definition Parolek and the paper this project adapts both use), EPCAD's figure drops to 9.15%, while the Census's own household survey (ACS) implies 16.96% actually exists. EPCAD's classification — a legal record of what's been permitted and assessed — captures only about half of the missing-middle housing independently gathered survey data says is really there. Section 5 has the full comparison; section 6 ties all four pieces of evidence together.

THE MODEL — LEARNING WHAT MISSING MIDDLE LOOKS LIKE STRUCTURALLY

A classifier trained only on confirmed missing-middle and confirmed single-family parcels — 36 features across three groups (structural: lot size, structure count, square footage, year built, distance downtown; building-shape/footprint; and ownership/financing, including First American's independent unit/building counts), with the EPCAD code that defines the label explicitly excluded — learns what missing-middle housing looks like structurally, then flags single-family-labeled parcels that fit the same signature. Test AUC: 0.996. Adding the shape and ownership features (an earlier pass used structural features alone) raised AUC from 0.986, but also shrank the candidate set it flags — a more precise classifier ends up more conservative, not more generous. See the callout below the candidate panel for what that traded off. Below are the descriptive statistics behind why it works, and what it flags.

SHARE BY LOT SIZE

Not a straight line — a U-shape. Share runs 47.8% on the smallest lots (under 1,500 sqft), bottoms out around 1.6% at the typical single-family lot size (4,500-8,700 sqft), then climbs to 41.2% on 87,100+ sqft parcels (2+ acres — large multi-family sites). The linear correlation alone (r = +0.197) would miss this shape entirely.

SHARE BY BUILDING SIZE

Same U-shape: 10.1% share at 600-900 sqft (casita-sized), down to 2.3% at 1500-1800 sqft, back up to 16.7% at 3500+ sqft (large apartment buildings). The linear read (r = +0.068) is weak and misleading on its own.

ZONING CODES MOST CONCENTRATED

Share of parcels within each code that are missing-middle (min. 100 parcels), using the City of El Paso's official zoning layer (clean ZONELABEL), not EPCAD's raw GIS field, which had inconsistent duplicate spellings for the same district.

OWNERSHIP SIGNAL — HOMESTEAD EXEMPTION

Texas's homestead exemption legally requires owner-occupancy — the strongest single correlate of missing-middle status found this round (r = -0.27).

WHAT "TEST AUC" ACTUALLY MEASURES — AND WHY 0.996 IS GOOD BUT NOT THE WHOLE STORY

AUC (area under the ROC curve) answers one specific question: if you picked one random confirmed missing-middle parcel and one random confirmed single-family parcel from the held-out test set (59,831 parcels the model never trained on), how often would the model rank the missing-middle one as more likely to be missing-middle? An AUC of 0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect separation. At 0.996, the model gets that ordering right 99.6% of the time — yes, that's a strong score, well above what most real-world classification tasks reach, and it means the structural/shape/ownership signature of missing-middle housing really is distinct from single-family housing in this data.

But AUC alone doesn't tell you how many candidates to flag — that depends on precision (of the parcels flagged missing-middle, how many actually are) and recall (of the parcels that actually are missing-middle, how many got flagged). At the 0.5 threshold used to generate candidates, this model scores 0.66 precision / 0.95 recall on the class that matters — it catches 95% of true missing-middle parcels in the test set, at the cost of one false positive for roughly every two true ones. That's a deliberate, reasonable trade-off for a screening tool meant to flag parcels worth a closer look, not a tool meant to make a final determination on its own.

None of this is the same question as "did the model find real undercounted missing-middle housing." AUC, precision, and recall are all computed against EPCAD's own labels — parcels the county has already classified. The candidates below are single-family-labeled parcels the model thinks share the missing-middle signature; EPCAD has made no determination on them one way or the other, so there's no ground truth to score the candidates against directly. The callout below, and section 5's ACS comparison, are the closest this project gets to an answer on that separate question — and the answer there is much more modest than the AUC alone would suggest.

BETTER CLASSIFIER, SMALLER CANDIDATE SET

Adding footprint-shape and ownership/financing features (First American's independent unit/building counts among them) raised test AUC from 0.986 to 0.996 and sharply improved precision — but also tightened the model's probability estimates, so fewer parcels clear each confidence threshold: candidates at p≥0.5 fell from 7,909 to 4,439, and high-confidence candidates (p≥0.8) fell from 2,622 to 1,569. Downstream, that smaller set closes slightly less of the section 5 EPCAD-vs-ACS gap than the noisier prior model did (88 of 182 tracts improved vs. 96 of 182). A classifier that's better at telling confirmed missing-middle from confirmed single-family apart isn't automatically better at guessing at the undercounted kind EPCAD never labeled at all — those are different questions, and this project reports both answers rather than the more flattering one.

VALIDATION, LAYER 1 — BUILDING FOOTPRINTS (EXTERNAL, GEOMETRIC)

The original Salazar-Miranda & Talen paper detects housing typology from a street-view CV model's read of building shape. El Paso has no imagery pipeline, but real building-footprint polygons (Microsoft GlobalMLBuildingFootprints) let us compute actual shape features and cross-check EPCAD's own structure counts against physical reality — independent of the classifier above.

FOOTPRINTS VS. RECORDED STRUCTURES

Of single-family parcels with footprint coverage, show more dwelling-sized footprints than EPCAD has recorded structures (a candidate undocumented ADU/casita), and show footprint area exceeding recorded square footage by >1.5x (a candidate undocumented addition). Caveat: footprint polygons are noisy (split roofs, oversized garages) — read this as a screening signal, not a confirmed count.

Broken out by typology (medians, since a few shared-building parcels skew the mean): duplexes run hot on the footprint-ratio signal (25.0% addition-flag rate vs. 2.3% for single-family) without the extreme outliers seen elsewhere — a real structural signature, not noise. Large apartment complexes (31-99 / 100+ units) show high new-detached rates (22% / 42%), expected since big complexes legitimately have many buildings per parcel. Townhouse/condo addition-flag rates (44% / 49%) are mostly a spatial-join artifact — a shared building's footprint sometimes attaches to one unit's small individually-recorded parcel — not real undocumented expansions; see data/shape_by_typology.json.

VALIDATION, LAYER 2 — ACS UNITS-IN-STRUCTURE (EXTERNAL, SURVEY-BASED)

The Census Bureau's ACS asks households directly how many units are in their building (table B25024), independent of any local permitting or assessment process — a third benchmark alongside EPCAD's parcel records and the footprint cross-check above. Whose count is closer to reality?

EPCAD VS. ACS UNITS-IN-STRUCTURE, BY CENSUS TRACT

Each dot is one of 182 census tracts. The chart below plots EPCAD's broad (any non-single-family structure) share against ACS — countywide, EPCAD (20.72%) and ACS (23.05%) look close, but that's misleading: EPCAD's broad flag includes large apartment complexes that aren't "missing middle" by any real definition, and the ACS side was scoped the same broad way to match it.

Scoped to missing middle's actual definition — 2-19 units, per Parolek and the Salazar-Miranda & Talen paper this project adapts — the picture changes substantially. EPCAD (9.15%) captures only about 54% of what ACS implies exists (16.96%) — a real 7.8-point countywide undercount, not a unit-of-analysis artifact. Tract-level mean absolute difference is 10.36 points. Here, unlike at any other definition tested, the structural model's high-confidence candidates help slightly more often than not — with the current (shape+ownership-feature) model, mean diff moves to 10.30, improving 88 of 182 tracts (49%); the prior, structural-only model moved it to 10.31, improving 96 of 182 (53%). Either way it's weak evidence, not decisive, and nowhere near enough to close a gap this size (ACS implies ~24,000 more missing-middle units exist than EPCAD has on record). WHAT THIS MEANS, PUT TOGETHER

Four independent pieces of evidence, each with its own blind spots, point the same direction. The legal mechanism (section 1): single-family zoning makes most missing-middle housing illegal to build, so owners have every reason not to document it. EPCAD's own data (sections 2-3) already shows missing-middle concentrated exactly where that mechanism predicts — near downtown, on oddly small or large lots, in older stock, strongly tied to non-owner-occupancy — a pattern clean enough that a structural model separates missing-middle from single-family parcels at 0.996 AUC using structure, shape, and ownership signal, never the EPCAD label itself. The footprint check (section 4), an entirely separate data source, independently confirms ~8.7% of single-family-labeled parcels carry more or larger structures than EPCAD has on record. The ACS check (section 5), a third and completely independent source, puts an actual number on the size of the gap: at missing middle's real definition, EPCAD accounts for only about half of what household survey data implies exists.

The key finding, reasoned through. Put those four pieces together and a specific explanation for where the undercount lives falls out — an inference from the pattern of results, not a new measurement on its own. A model trained purely on structure, footprint shape, and ownership signal — never on EPCAD's own classification — can tell confirmed missing-middle parcels from confirmed single-family parcels apart at 0.996 AUC (section 3): converted missing-middle housing leaves a detectable structural fingerprint even with no permit on file. But two separate exterior-evidence methods built to catch that fingerprint — the footprint-shape check (section 4, ~8.7% of single-family parcels flagged) and the model's high-confidence candidates (section 3, 1,569-2,622 parcels) — both undershoot the ACS-implied ~24,000-unit gap (section 5) by roughly an order of magnitude. The straightforward reading: most of the undercounted missing-middle housing was converted without any exterior change — internal subdivisions, a garage or basement turned into a separate unit, no new footprint or roofline — which is exactly the kind of conversion no satellite comparison or permit record would ever catch. This project has no interior-inspection or permit-matching data to confirm it directly, but it's the explanation consistent with every method here without requiring any of them to be wrong.

That has three specific implications. First, single-family homes can reasonably be converted to missing-middle housing, but for zoning and building codes — the conversions are evidently feasible, since the pattern above implies they're already happening; what's missing is the legal framework from section 1, not a technical or market barrier. Second, these renovations don't require changes that would alter a neighborhood's exterior character — if they did, the two exterior-evidence methods would have found far more of them than they did. Third, this data can help target where zoning and code reform would matter most, because the concentration pattern in sections 2-3 (near downtown, older housing stock, non-owner-occupied) identifies specific tracts where informal conversion is evidently already happening, ahead of any policy change.

What this project does not show: that the structural candidate detector has found the missing units, or that internal conversion is the only driver of the gap. Some of it is almost certainly EPCAD assessment lag or genuine ACS sampling noise at the tract level — this project can't cleanly separate how much of the ~24,000-unit gap is real but invisible conversion versus measurement noise between two different data sources. Confirming the internal-conversion explanation specifically would take field verification or a different kind of evidence entirely (permit-vs-utility-hookup cross-referencing, interior inspection, resident surveys) that this project's geometry- and classification-based methods can't provide on their own.

For policy purposes, the practical takeaway is the one at the top of this page: El Paso almost certainly has substantially more missing-middle housing than its own records show, concentrated in specific, identifiable places — inner-ring, pre-war, non-owner-occupied tracts — and the best-supported explanation for why it's invisible to both administrative records and satellite-based detection is that it's happening inside existing buildings, not through additions or new construction that would already have been visible. Already happening, already low-impact on neighborhood character, already concentrated in identifiable places — that's the case for treating this as a legalization and code-reform question rather than a detection problem to keep refining.
