Missing-middle housing — duplexes, triplexes, courtyard apartments, small multi-unit buildings mixed into single-family blocks — almost certainly exists in greater quantity than official county records show, because across most of El Paso it is not legal to build.

Single-family zoning covers the overwhelming majority of residential land, so an informal second unit, a converted garage, or a backyard casita has every incentive to stay off the books. A property owner who adds a unit doesn't file a permit for something zoning forbids.

That makes the central appraisal district's own classification — built from permitted, assessed improvements — a floor, not a ceiling, on how much missing-middle housing actually exists.

Here's where it starts. EPCAD's own records say 21.56% of El Paso's housing units are missing-middle — but most of that is large apartment complexes, not missing middle in the term's real sense.

Scoped correctly — 2–19 units, the definition Parolek and the paper this project adapts both use — EPCAD's own figure drops to 10.11%.

The Census's own household survey (ACS) — residents self-reporting how many units are in their building, independent of any permit or assessment — implies 16.96% actually exists at that same scoped definition.

EPCAD's classification captures only about 60% of the missing-middle housing independently gathered survey data says is really there.

The rest of this page sizes that gap from five independent angles: EPCAD's own baseline, a structural model trained to spot what EPCAD's classification might miss, an external geometric check against real building footprints, an external survey check against Census data, and an external address-level check against El Paso Electric's own meter billing records. Section 7 ties all five together; the final section maps where that gap points to reform.

READING EPCAD'S OWN RECORDS

El Paso Central Appraisal District (EPCAD) already publishes an authoritative, deterministic typology per parcel from its STATE_CD/ClassCD fields — no model needed to read it. Every dot on the map is a parcel EPCAD itself already calls missing-middle.

A FIX WORTH EXPLAINING

EPCAD's STATE_CD does track triplexes and fourplexes separately — codes B3 and B4 — but an earlier version of this pipeline lumped B3, B4, B5, and B6 together into one "Multi-Unit Residential (Other)" bucket and, worse, counted every parcel in that bucket as exactly one unit in every unit-weighted statistic on this page. A real triplex was being counted the same as a studio apartment.

That's now fixed: B3 is split out as Triplex (368 parcels, 3 units each) and B4 as Fourplex (906 parcels, 4 units each) — based on the Texas statewide SPTB state-code convention other Texas appraisal districts document for these same codes, corroborated here by B3/B4 parcels averaging more improvement records per parcel than B1 duplexes do. B5 (125 parcels) and B6 (24) stay lumped as "Multi-Unit Residential (Other)" — too few parcels and too ambiguous a structural signal to confidently assign them a specific unit count (EPCAD's own site blocks automated lookups, so this couldn't be confirmed against a published legend directly).

Fixing this wasn't cosmetic — this page's headline undercount numbers moved. Countywide unit-weighted missing-middle share went from 20.72% to 21.56%; at the scoped 2–19-unit definition (section 5), EPCAD's recorded share went from 9.15% to 10.11% of what ACS implies exists — closing roughly a point of the undercount gap just by counting units in parcels that were already correctly flagged as missing-middle, just under-weighted.

THE MODEL — LEARNING WHAT MISSING MIDDLE LOOKS LIKE STRUCTURALLY

A classifier trained only on confirmed missing-middle and confirmed single-family parcels — 37 features across four groups (structural: lot size, structure count, square footage, year built, distance downtown; building-shape/footprint; ownership/financing, including First American's independent unit/building counts; and physical capacity, specifically First American's total bathroom count — a house needs enough existing plumbing to split before it's worth converting), with the EPCAD code that defines the label explicitly excluded — learns what missing-middle housing looks like structurally, then flags single-family-labeled parcels that fit the same signature. Test AUC: 0.997. Adding the shape, ownership, and bathroom-count features (an earlier pass used structural features alone) raised AUC from 0.986, but also shrank the candidate set it flags — a more precise classifier ends up more conservative, not more generous. See the callout below the candidate panel for what that traded off. Below are the descriptive statistics behind why it works, and what it flags.

SHARE BY LOT SIZE

Not a straight line — a U-shape. Share runs 47.8% on the smallest lots (under 1,500 sqft), bottoms out around 1.6% at the typical single-family lot size (4,500-8,700 sqft), then climbs to 41.2% on 87,100+ sqft parcels (2+ acres — large multi-family sites). The linear correlation alone (r = +0.197) would miss this shape entirely.

THREE QUESTIONS, NOT A CONTRADICTION

It's easy to read "0.997 AUC" next to "0.66 precision" and hear the model described as both great and not-great at the same time. It isn't a contradiction — they're answers to three separate questions, and all three can be true at once.

Q1 — can it tell them apart at all? Show it one confirmed missing-middle parcel and one confirmed single-family parcel, no threshold involved, just rank them. It gets the ranking right 99.7% of the time.

Q2 — where do you draw the line? To turn that ranking into an actual candidate list, you have to pick a cutoff. This project uses p ≥ 0.5 — a deliberately loose bar, chosen to flag more parcels worth a second look rather than fewer. A stricter cutoff (p ≥ 0.8) produces a shorter, higher-confidence list instead; see the candidate panel below.

Q3 — how good is the list at that line? At p ≥ 0.5: 95% recall (it catches 95% of true missing-middle parcels in the test set) but only 66% precision (1 in 3 flagged parcels is a false positive). A loose cutoff chosen to maximize recall will always cost precision — that's the direct, mechanical consequence of the choice made in Q2, not a flaw in the model.

THE ANALOGY

Think of a smoke detector tuned to go off at the faintest hint of smoke. It will catch nearly every real fire (high recall) — but it will also go off for burnt toast fairly often (lower precision). A less sensitive detector would miss more real fires in exchange for fewer false alarms. Here, the candidate list is a screening tool meant to flag parcels worth a closer look, not a final determination — so this project deliberately tunes toward catching more real missing-middle parcels, accepting the false-positive cost that comes with it.

One more thing none of these three numbers answer: did the model find real undercounted missing-middle housing. AUC, precision, and recall are all scored against EPCAD's own labels — parcels the county has already classified one way or the other. The candidates below are single-family-labeled parcels the model thinks share the missing-middle signature; EPCAD has made no determination on them, so there's no ground truth to score against directly. Section 5's ACS comparison is the closest this project gets to an answer on that separate question — and the answer there is much more modest than the AUC alone would suggest.

BETTER CLASSIFIER, SMALLER CANDIDATE SET

Adding footprint-shape and ownership/financing features (First American's independent unit/building counts among them) raised test AUC from 0.986 to 0.996 and sharply improved precision — but also tightened the model's probability estimates, so fewer parcels clear each confidence threshold: candidates at p≥0.5 fell from 7,909 to 4,439, and high-confidence candidates (p≥0.8) fell from 2,622 to 1,569. Downstream, that smaller set closes slightly less of the section 5 EPCAD-vs-ACS gap than the noisier prior model did (88 of 182 tracts improved vs. 96 of 182). A classifier that's better at telling confirmed missing-middle from confirmed single-family apart isn't automatically better at guessing at the undercounted kind EPCAD never labeled at all — those are different questions, and this project reports both answers rather than the more flattering one.

The current model adds one more field on top of that v2 baseline: First American's total bathroom count — a physical-capacity signal (a house needs enough existing plumbing to split before a conversion is worth doing), ranking #5 in feature importance. It nudges test AUC to 0.997 and shrinks the candidate set a bit further (4,439 → 4,072 at p≥0.5; 1,569 → 1,471 at p≥0.8), and on the section 5 downstream test it's essentially a wash (84 of 182 tracts improved either way, mean diff 10.22 → 10.19). It's kept anyway: the ACS tract comparison only tests whether a parcel looks undercounted today, not whether it's physically capable of being converted tomorrow — a question the bathroom count speaks to that the downstream test was never built to measure.

WHOSE COUNT IS CLOSER TO REALITY?

The Census Bureau's ACS asks households directly how many units are in their building (table B25024), independent of any local permitting or assessment process — a third benchmark alongside EPCAD's parcel records and the footprint cross-check above.

Plotting EPCAD's broad (any non-single-family structure) share against ACS — countywide, EPCAD (21.56%) and ACS (23.05%) look close, but that's still somewhat misleading: EPCAD's broad flag includes large apartment complexes that aren't "missing middle" by any real definition, and the ACS side was scoped the same broad way to match it.

Scoped to missing middle's actual definition — 2–19 units, per Parolek and the Salazar-Miranda & Talen paper this project adapts — the picture changes substantially. EPCAD (10.11%) captures only about 60% of what ACS implies exists (16.96%) — a real 6.85-point countywide undercount, not a unit-of-analysis artifact (down from a 7.8-point/54% gap before this project split EPCAD's B3/B4 "triplex"/"fourplex" parcels out of a catch-all bucket that had been counting every one of them as a single unit — see section 2). Tract-level mean absolute difference is 10.22 points.

Here, unlike at any other definition tested, the structural model's high-confidence candidates help slightly more often than not — with the current (shape+ownership+bathroom-feature) model, mean diff moves to 10.19, improving 84 of 182 tracts (46%); the prior model (before the B3/B4 fix) had moved it to 10.30, improving 88 of 182 (49%). Either way it's weak evidence, not decisive, and nowhere near enough to close a gap this size (ACS implies ~21,000 more missing-middle units exist than EPCAD has on record). El Paso Electric classifies every service address into a billing class — Residential Single, Residential Duplex, Residential Triplex, or Apartment Complex — for rate-setting purposes, entirely independent of EPCAD's parcel records or this project's model. Unlike the footprint and ACS checks above, which only validate aggregate counts, this is the first source that can be joined address-by-address to the structural model's specific flagged parcels.

The join itself: EPE's data has no parcel ID or coordinates — only a free-text service address and ZIP — so this is a normalized-address string join against EPCAD's situs address fields. 58.8% of EPCAD's residential parcels (145,094 of 246,950) matched; the rest didn't match, most likely because an address didn't normalize identically on both sides, not because no meter exists.

Sanity check first: of the 8,739 parcels EPCAD's own STATE_CD field already calls missing-middle, 4,219 matched to an EPE record, and EPE's independent billing class agrees they're multi-unit for only 22.8% of them.

That's lower than it sounds like it should be, but it has a real, previously-known explanation rather than being a join-quality problem: EPE bills by electric account, not by inspected unit count — a duplex with one owner-paid account for the whole building reads as "Residential Single" to EPE even though it's a confirmed two-unit building. Of EPCAD-confirmed missing-middle parcels EPE calls "Residential Single," 2,142 are duplexes, 502 are townhouses, and 400 are fourplexes: EPE agreement is a floor on multi-unit status, not the real rate.

The actual test: for the structural model's 1,471 high-confidence (p ≥ 0.8) candidates — EPCAD single-family parcels the model thinks are secretly multi-unit — 775 (52.7%) matched an EPE record, and of those, 128 (16.5%) are independently billed by EPE as duplex, triplex, or apartment-complex rather than single. Among all EPCAD single-family parcels matched to EPE, that rate is only 0.76% — the model's high-confidence candidates hit an independent, address-level multi-unit signal at 21.7x the baseline rate.

Caveat, to be direct about it: 16.5% is a floor, not the model's true hit rate — the same billing-account undercount logic above means plenty of real hidden units never show up as a multi-unit EPE class even when they exist. But a 21.7x lift over baseline, from a data source with every incentive to under-, not over-, report multi-unit billing, is hard to explain away as coincidence. See data/epe_meter_validation.json and data/epe_confirmed_hidden_units.csv for the full figures and the 128 confirmed addresses.

TOGETHER, THEY POINT TO ONE EXPLANATION
THE KEY FINDING, REASONED THROUGH

Put those five pieces together and a specific explanation for where the undercount lives falls out — an inference from the pattern of results, not a new measurement on its own. A model trained purely on structure, footprint shape, ownership, and bathroom-count signal — never on EPCAD's own classification — can tell confirmed missing-middle parcels from confirmed single-family parcels apart at 0.997 AUC (section 3): converted missing-middle housing leaves a detectable structural fingerprint even with no permit on file. But two separate exterior-evidence methods built to catch that fingerprint — the footprint-shape check (~8.7% of single-family parcels flagged) and the model's high-confidence candidates (1,471-2,622 parcels across model versions) — both undershoot the ACS-implied ~21,000-unit gap by roughly an order of magnitude.

The straightforward reading: most of the undercounted missing-middle housing was converted without any exterior change — internal subdivisions, a garage or basement turned into a separate unit, no new footprint or roofline — exactly the kind of conversion no satellite comparison or permit record would ever catch. This project has no interior-inspection or permit-matching data to confirm it directly, but it's the explanation consistent with every method here without requiring any of them to be wrong. The electric-meter check (section 6) adds one more data point in the same direction: it independently confirms a real slice (128 parcels, a 21.7x lift over baseline) of the model's candidates specifically, without being able to confirm or rule out the rest — exactly what you'd expect if the candidates are mostly real but EPE's billing-account classification misses most of them the same way every other source here undercounts in one direction.

WHICH MEANS
THREE IMPLICATIONS

First — single-family homes can reasonably be converted to missing-middle housing, but for zoning and building codes. The conversions are evidently feasible, since the pattern above implies they're already happening; what's missing is the legal framework from section 1, not a technical or market barrier.

Second — these renovations don't require changes that would alter a neighborhood's exterior character. If they did, the two exterior-evidence methods would have found far more of them than they did.

Third — this data can help target where zoning and code reform would matter most, because the concentration pattern in sections 2-3 (near downtown, older housing stock, non-owner-occupied) identifies specific tracts where informal conversion is evidently already happening, ahead of any policy change.

WHAT THIS PROJECT DOES NOT SHOW

That the structural candidate detector has found the missing units, or that internal conversion is the only driver of the gap. Some of it is almost certainly EPCAD assessment lag or genuine ACS sampling noise at the tract level — this project can't cleanly separate how much of the ~24,000-unit gap is real but invisible conversion versus measurement noise between two different data sources. Confirming the internal-conversion explanation specifically would take field verification or a different kind of evidence entirely (permit-vs-utility-hookup cross-referencing, interior inspection, resident surveys) that this project's geometry- and classification-based methods can't provide on their own.

FOR POLICY PURPOSES

El Paso almost certainly has substantially more missing-middle housing than its own records show, concentrated in specific, identifiable places — inner-ring, pre-war, non-owner-occupied tracts — and the best-supported explanation for why it's invisible to both administrative records and satellite-based detection is that it's happening inside existing buildings, not through additions or new construction that would already have been visible. Already happening, already low-impact on neighborhood character, already concentrated in identifiable places — that's the case for treating this as a legalization and code-reform question rather than a detection problem to keep refining.
